# MoE at 0.8B total (drafted 2026-09-05, user direction via 4c 09:50 local)

## Two design constraints as given are not jointly satisfiable, and this is the first thing to settle

**The ask carries an arithmetic conflict.** Equal active FFN params (FLOP parity with the
control) plus ~0.8B total plus "~20-25% activation" cannot all hold *when every layer is MoE*.
At equal-active the total is pinned by the sparsity, because the active FFN is fixed at the
control's 113,246,208:

| FFN activation | FFN pool | TOTAL params |
|---|---|---|
| 25% | 0.453B | 0.546B |
| 20% | 0.566B | 0.659B |
| **16.0%** | **0.708B** | **0.801B** |
| 5% | 2.265B | 2.358B |

All computed, not assumed: control FFN is SwiGLU `w13` (2·d·ffn) + `w2` (ffn·d) = 3·1024·3072 =
9,437,184 per layer × 12 = 113,246,208, which is 54.9% of the control's 206,128,200 dense
parameters; non-FFN (embeddings, attention, norms, head) = 92,881,992 and is unchanged in every
arm. So with all 12 layers MoE, `total = 92,881,992 + 113,246,208 / activation`.

**The layer count is the third free variable, and it dissolves the conflict.** 4c's ruling (2) is
right that the charter must name which layers are MoE; converting only some of them decouples
total capacity from activation. Every cell below is FLOP-matched — active FFN per MoE layer is
4 × 3·1024·768 = 9,437,184, exactly the dense FFN it replaces — so these differ only in TOTAL
capacity, not in compute:

| MoE layers | N=16 | N=24 | N=32 | N=48 | N=64 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | 0.329B | 0.404B | 0.480B | 0.631B | 0.782B |
| 6 | 0.390B | 0.503B | 0.617B | **0.843B** | 1.070B |
| 8 | 0.451B | 0.602B | 0.753B | 1.055B | 1.357B |
| 12 | 0.574B | **0.801B** | 1.027B | 1.480B | 1.933B |

**One correction to 4c's ruling (2), computed, and withdrawn by 4c 2026-09-05:** "6 of 12 lands
~0.8B total" does not hold at 33 experts. 6 MoE layers × (32 routed + 1 shared) × 2,359,296 +
6 dense FFN × 9,437,184 + 92,881,992 non-FFN = **0.617B**, not ~0.8B. 4c read the recomputation
and withdrew the figure the same day. The 4c arithmetic that is right is the other half: 12 layers
× 33 experts is 934.3M of FFN alone → 1.027B total, correctly ruled out. Reaching ~0.8B needs
either **6 layers × 48 routed (0.843B)** or **12 layers × 24 routed (0.801B)**.

Those two are a real design choice and I will not make it silently:

- **E1 = 12 MoE layers × 24 routed + shared, 0.801B.** Every layer sparse, 25 experts each.
  Closest to 0.8B, and the routing question gets asked at every depth.
- **E1b = 6 MoE layers × 48 routed + shared, 0.843B.** Half the layers dense, 49 experts in the
  sparse ones. Which 6 must be named — I would use the ODD layers (1,3,5,7,9,11) rather than a
  contiguous half, so no two MoE layers are adjacent and the memory pool's layer-6 result stays
  comparable; a contiguous choice confounds depth with sparsity.

**I recommend E1, and I am correcting my own first answer here.** My initial reasoning for E1b was
that halving the routers halves the things that can collapse and "doubles tokens per router" —
the first is true, the second is the wrong quantity. Computed: at 262,144 tokens per step with
top-3, E1 gives **32,768 tokens per routed expert per step** and E1b gives **16,384**, because
E1b has twice as many experts in each sparse layer. Per-expert token count is what the aux-loss-free
bias update has to work from, so E1b halves the signal on exactly the mechanism that has to prevent
the collapse we just measured. E1 is 0.801B (the total the user named) and gives each expert twice
the tokens. E1b remains worth running as the layer-count axis, but not as the primary.

**And 5% at 0.8B total is a different experiment, not a variant.** Holding total at 0.8B while
activating 5% means the active FFN is 35.4M, not 113.2M — 0.31× the control's FFN width. Its
FLOP-matched dense control is `ffn_hidden` 959, i.e. **128.2M total parameters, not the ~100M in
the direction**. That control does not exist and would have to be trained (3815 steps, one more
run). So the 5% variant costs two runs, not one, and its result is not comparable to the primary
arm's.

## What I would push back on before this becomes a row

**The routing-collapse risk is not hypothetical here — we measured it eight hours ago.** M1's
product-key selector collapsed to 9.45% of its rows by step 1000 with key_gini 0.9192
(`facts/memory_layers.json#mem.m1_key_usage_collapse`), and the mechanism was that a learned
selector at the same lr as the thing it selects concentrates. An MoE router is the same shape of
object: a small learned map choosing among many parameter blocks. Aux-loss-free bias updates are
a stronger defence than product keys had, and DeepSeek-V3 reports them working at scale — but
**that scale is now a measured number, not an adjective: 1,966,080 tokens per routed expert per
step** (15,360 sequences × 4096 × 8 active / 256 routed, computed from their reported batch
schedule; `facts/moe.json#moe.dsv3_bias_update_speed`). At world 2,
batch 16, accum 2, seq 4096 = 262,144 tokens per step with top-3 routing: **32,768 tokens per
routed expert per step at E1** (24 experts) and **16,384 at E1b** (48), before any imbalance —
**a factor of 60.0 below the regime their γ = 0.001 was reported in, and 12.0× below even their
warmup batch** — and the accum-2 split means the bias update sees half of that per backward. That
is thin, and it is the regime where the bias update has least signal. It is also the reason E1 is
the primary and not E1b: more experts per layer means fewer tokens each, on the mechanism that has
to hold.

The per-SEQUENCE picture runs the other way, and it is worth stating because it is the one place we
are better off than the paper: their complementary sequence-wise balance loss (α = 1e-4,
`facts/moe.json#moe.dsv3_seq_balance_alpha`) reads 4096 × 8 / 256 = **128** tokens per expert per
sequence, while E1 gives **512**. So α's regime is 4× better than theirs while γ's is 60× worse —
which says the per-sequence term is the more transferable of the two defences, not that either is
established here.

One quantitative difference from the memory case, in the MoE's favour and worth stating because it
is the reason to expect a different outcome: M1's selector chose 32 rows out of 1,048,576 —
3.05e-5 of the pool per token — so a row could go unread for many steps and never be corrected.
E1's router chooses 3 of 24, i.e. 12.5% of experts per token, and the bias update acts on every
expert every step whether or not it was chosen. The failure mode is the same shape; the correction
frequency differs by 4.10e+03 — call it three to four orders, computed as
0.125 / 3.052e-5. That is a reason for a stop rule rather than a reason to skip one.

So the honest statement is: **the failure we just measured is the failure most likely to recur, and
the primary defence is reported at 60× our per-expert token count.** Readout 4 below is
therefore a stop rule, not a diagnostic, and I would rather it fire early than have a second arm
stopped at step 1000.

**The conversion-rate readout as specified cannot be built on code.** The direction says "3b's
novel_ops sets" on code+math. Measured: `facts/corpus_supply.json#cs.code_tests_supply` is 15
novel rows / **0 tokens** — there is no `data/corpus/code_tests`, only a yield study that writes
`result.json` and no shards. The math side is fine (1,342 generator functions across 25
`math_programs_l*.py`, all randomised, instances effectively unlimited). So this readout is
**math-only** unless a miner run happens first, and planning it as code+math would plan against
supply that does not exist.

## Optimizer routing, pre-registered not tuned (4c ruling (f), 2026-09-05)

Three separate decisions, each fixed before launch so none of them becomes a knob turned after
seeing a curve:

1. **Experts stay in Muon, exactly as the dense FFN they replace.** `build_optimizers` routes 2D
   matrices to Muon and the expert weights are 2D, so this is the DEFAULT path and no exclusion
   is written. The reason is the experiment's validity, not convenience: if the experts trained
   under a different optimizer than the FFN they replace, the arm would differ from the control in
   sparsity AND in optimizer, and a loss delta could not be attributed. The memory program routed
   its table OUT of Muon for a reason that does not apply here — a sparse COO gradient that Muon's
   Newton-Schulz cannot consume. Expert gradients are dense.
   - Stated as an open risk rather than settled: whether Newton-Schulz orthogonalisation behaves
     on 50 matrices per MoE layer (25 `w13` of shape (1536,1024) and 25 `w2` of (1024,768)), each
     seeing a variable token count, is unestablished.
     It is the same operation the dense FFN already gets, which is the argument for it; a
     collapsed expert receiving near-zero tokens for a window is a case the dense FFN never
     presents, which is the argument against. Readout 4 is what would show it.
2. **The router goes to AdamW at `attn_res_lr` (0.01)**, via `--moe_router_lr`, as a
   PRE-REGISTERED CONSTANT TAKEN FROM AN EXISTING GROUP and **not derived from the expert group**.
   `attn_res_lr` is this repo's AdamW rate for a small learned MIXING MAP — AttnRes's dynamic
   pseudo-query weights — which is the closest analogue here to a router, so no new lr enters the
   arm. Ruled by 4c 2026-09-05.

   **CORRECTION, and the earlier version of this passage was wrong in a way worth recording.** It
   said the router must never take "a memory-style 20× lr" and glossed that as "mem_lr 0.02 = 20×
   the dense 1.00e-3". Both halves fail. **No lr in this codebase is 1.00e-3** — the complete set
   is `muon_lr` 0.01, `embed_lr` 0.1, `scalar_lr` 0.15, `attn_res_lr` 0.01, `mem_lr` 0.02,
   `head_lr` 0.0, and the control ran `lr_scale 1.0` so nothing rescales into it. The real
   figures are **`mem_lr` 0.02 against `muon_lr` 0.01**, and they sit in **different optimizers**
   (Adagrad and Muon), so a ratio between them is not a meaningful multiple — "equal to the expert
   rate" was never a well-formed comparison either, for the same reason. The 20× came from the
   brief this charter was written against and was carried into the text without being checked;
   tilerl caught the consequence (a `muon_lr` default routes the router to the expert group's rate,
   which ruling (f) excludes) and the recomputation found the premise.

   **What the cited fact actually says**, and it is all this charter may lean on:
   `facts/memory_layers.json#mem.m1_key_usage_collapse` records ONE Adagrad group holding all six
   memory tensors at `mem_lr` 0.02, and a selection distribution concentrating to 9.45% of the
   pool by step 1000 (key_gini 0.9192). It records **no dense-lr ratio at all**. The separate toy
   sweep moved usage 0.078 → 1.000 across two orders of magnitude of selector lr, which is the
   evidence that a selector's lr matters — not evidence for any particular multiple.

   The balance mechanism is the **bias update**, not the router's lr, and **readout 4 catches both
   directions**: a collapse (usage below 0.50 at step 300) and a flat load that never specialises
   (entropy pinned near 1.0 with no differentiation). The flag exists so the value is recorded in
   the launch line and the checkpoint cfg rather than living in a default nobody reads — the same
   reason `--mem_sel_lr` exists (8c23ea86).
3. **The aux-loss-free bias is NOT a gradient and has its own step size**, updated per step from
   each expert's load rather than by backprop. **RESOLVED 2026-09-05 by reading the paper:
   γ = 0.001**, pre-registered as OUR value borrowed from
   `facts/moe.json#moe.dsv3_bias_update_speed` (arXiv:2412.19437 §2.1.2 for the rule, §4.2 for the
   value). Two things come with it:
   - **The scale gap is registered with the value.** Our E1 arm gives 32,768 tokens per routed
     expert per step against their 1,966,080 at the steady-state batch — a factor of **60.0**, and
     12.0× below even their warmup batch. γ is a step size on a control loop whose input is a
     per-expert token count, and that count's relative noise grows as it shrinks. Borrowing a
     starting point, not inheriting a result. Their γ also has a schedule we are not copying
     (0.001 for 14.3T tokens then 0.0 for the last 500B); our 1B-token run sits entirely inside
     their first phase, so constant 0.001 is the faithful reading.
   - **The bias enters top-k SELECTION only.** "Note that the bias term is only used for routing";
     the gating value multiplied into the FFN output uses the UN-BIASED affinity score. Biasing the
     gate too changes the function the arm computes, not just its routing, so the module's test
     asserts the un-biased gate directly.
4. **A complementary sequence-wise balance loss at α = 1e-4 is ON in every arm**
   (`facts/moe.json#moe.dsv3_seq_balance_alpha`, arXiv:2412.19437 eq. 17). DeepSeek-V3's design is
   **not purely aux-loss-free** — the bias handles balance across the step's batch and this term
   handles extremes within one sequence. Its scale argument runs the OTHER way: per sequence at
   seq 4096 we get 512 tokens per expert against their 128, so α's regime is 4× better than theirs
   while γ's is 60× worse. Not an arm axis; on, at 1e-4, everywhere.

**Neither constant is tuned after seeing a curve.** If readout 4's stop rule fires, the report says
the balancer failed at this scale with the paper's γ. It does not retune γ and relaunch — that
makes the arm unattributable and turns a measurement into a search. A γ sweep, if ever warranted,
is a separate registered row.

It stayed blank for a while on purpose: 4c's ruling asked for DeepSeek-V3's value with the paper

failure this repo has paid for repeatedly — a number with a citation nobody read. The resolution was
the cheap one: read it. **γ = 0.001** (§4.2), the update rule and its routing-only scope (§2.1.2),
plus the complementary sequence-wise balance loss at **α = 1e-4** that the "aux-loss-free" label
hides. Both are now in `facts/moe.json` as `recorded` — paper-reported, not measured here — each
carrying the 60× per-expert-token gap in its own boundary field, and both are pre-registered as OUR
borrowed values in `runs/prereg.jsonl#moe_0905`. See the optimizer section above for what that
borrowing does and does not license.

Each is a measurement, not an opinion. **They are no longer a launch gate:** amendment 4 struck
readout 0, so questions 1-4 are ANSWERED, not pending -- tilerl measured them on card 7
(`facts/efficiency.json`, a97b9c74): _scaled_grouped_mm runs at our per-expert sizes across 11
group sizes, one compile over 12 steps of changing routing, and three API constraints now encoded
in `MoEFFN`. Question 5 stays open and is answered by the arm itself: with all experts resident at
world 2, an imbalanced step costs the max-loaded expert's time, so readouts 4 and 5 can fire on one
underlying cause and the report must say which.

1. **Grouped GEMM at 25-49 small experts, world 2.** At E1 the per-expert GEMM is
   [tokens_routed × 1024] × [1024 × 1536] (w13) — 32,768 tokens per expert per step, but split
   across accum 2 and taken per layer, so the per-call M is ~16,384 at best and far less under
   imbalance. Is `grouped_mm` available and fp8-survivable in this stack, or does it become 25
   separate small GEMMs per layer? The ratio of that to one dense [·×1024]×[1024×6144] is the
   throughput readout's whole content.
2. **Dispatch cost.** Token permutation/gather to expert-contiguous order and the inverse
   scatter, per layer per step. Measured ms, the way the memory lookup's 51.6 ms of a 799 ms
   step was measured — not a FLOP argument, since this is a memory-movement cost.
3. **fp8 interaction.** Expert weights are `nn.Linear` and 16-aligned at w=768/384, so
   `convert_to_float8_training` WILL reach them unless excluded by fqn. Does per-tensor e4m3
   scaling behave on a matrix that sees a variable, sometimes tiny, token count per step? The
   memory work excluded its projections because fp8 changes WHICH values a discrete top-k
   selects; the same question applies to the router logits and is sharper there — a routing flip
   is a discrete change in which parameters a token reaches, not a small numerical one.
4. **torch.compile recompiles.** The memory lookup traced in one graph with 0 breaks but
   Inductor's C++ backend failed on a laptop toolchain
   (`probes/mem_toy.py::report_compile`) — and reporting that as a graph break would have sent
   the reader after a defect the trace says is not there, so the bench should separate the trace
   from the backend. A router has data-dependent shapes, the classic recompile trigger; count
   recompilations over 50 steps, not just presence/absence.
5. **Load imbalance as a wall-clock cost.** With ranks holding all experts (no expert
   parallelism at world 2), an imbalanced step costs the max-loaded expert's time, so imbalance
   is a throughput term and not only a quality term — the two stop rules (readout 4 and readout
   5) can therefore fire on the same underlying cause, and the report must say which it was.
   tilerl's five-cell decomposition method is the right instrument.

## What would change the design

- If **readout 5 on the arm** reads below 0.85 of the dense control at step 30, the arm is stopped
  and the move is toward FEWER, WIDER experts — k=3/w=768 with N=16 (0.574B total, all 12 layers)
  keeps ratio 1.000 and raises tokens-per-expert to 49,152 — not toward a tuned coefficient. The
  0.85 bar is unchanged; what moved (amendment 4) is WHERE it is read. It was a pre-launch bench
  gate; the bench produces no tok/s/gpu, so the bar now applies at step 30 of the arm, which is the
  first 10-step window and costs minutes rather than a card-hour. The memory program's lesson
  stands — it spent two cards on a 0.79 shape — and step 30 is early enough to serve it.
- If a 100-step smoke shows usage fraction below 0.50 before step 100, the bias-update defence is
  not working at this token-per-expert scale and the design needs a different balancer before a
  full run, not a tuned coefficient.
- If the user prefers 20-25% activation with all 12 layers MoE, the total drops to 0.55-0.66B and
  E1 becomes N=19 k=3 w=768 (0.659B) — same instruments, different row.
- If the microbench shows fp8 flipping routing decisions (question 3), the router logits leave
  fp8 by fqn and that exclusion is asserted in a test before launch, the way the memory
  program's three projections are. A routing flip changes which parameters a token reaches;
  it is not a tolerance question.
